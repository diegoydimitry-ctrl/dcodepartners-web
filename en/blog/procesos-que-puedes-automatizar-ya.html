5 processes in your company you could probably automate right now

D-Code Partners

You don't need a huge project to start automating. Most companies have two or three "silent" processes that eat up hours every week without anyone stopping to question them, simply because that's how it's always been done. Here are five of the most common ones.

1. Sorting and filing documents

Invoices, contracts, forms received by email or uploaded to a shared folder. If someone on your team spends time opening them, figuring out what they are, and moving them to the right place, that's a clear candidate: a system can read the content, classify it, and file it directly.

2. Answering repeated customer questions

Hours, pricing, availability, order status. If your customer service team keeps answering the same five or ten questions, most of that time can be freed up with an agent that resolves them instantly, leaving the complex queries for people.

3. Gathering data for recurring reports

The weekly or monthly report someone puts together by copying numbers from several different sources into a spreadsheet. If the data already exists in some system, that process can almost always be automated end to end, including sending out the final report.

4. Following up on leads and pending quotes

That prospect someone "needed to write back to" and got forgotten. Sales reminders and follow-ups are among the processes with the best ratio of automation effort to revenue impact, precisely because the cost of not doing them is directly a lost sale.

5. Onboarding new clients or employees

The welcome sequence, the documents that need to be collected, the accounts that need to be activated. It's a process that repeats with every new person, with nearly identical steps each time — exactly the kind of task that should least depend on someone remembering to do it manually.

How to take the next step

If you recognized your business in two or more of these points, you don't have to decide alone where to start. An outside diagnosis usually turns up additional processes that, seen from the inside, no longer even register as "a problem" — they're just "how it's always been done."
